2. Materials and Methods

2.1 Animals
Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats weighing 200–250 g were obtained from BioLASCO Co., Ltd. (Taipei, Taiwan) and used in this study. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Taipei Medical University (approval no. LAC-2021-0259). The animals were cared for and handled in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health for the care and use of laboratory animals.

2.2 Unilateral ureteral obstruction rat model
  Renal fibrosis was induced using a UUO rat model. Briefly, rats were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil (40 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and placed on a temperature-controlled operating table to maintain body temperature at 37 °C. After abdominal skin preparation, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the right proximal ureter. The ureter was ligated at two separate sites using 3–0 black silk sutures (684G; Ethicon Inc., Johnson & Johnson, Bridgewater, NJ, USA). Sham-operated rats underwent the same surgical procedure, including exposure of the right ureter, but without ureteral ligation.
  At 7 days after surgery, the right kidneys were harvested from both UUO and sham-operated rats. The obstructed right kidneys from UUO rats and the corresponding right kidneys from sham-operated rats were processed for histopathological and transmission electron microscopic examination, and the remaining renal tissues were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. Blood samples were collected by aortic puncture and centrifuged to obtain serum, which was stored at −80 °C until analysis. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and serum creatinine (Scr) levels were measured using a Vitros 750 autoanalyzer (Johnson & Johnson, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) to assess renal function.

2.3 Histopathological examination 
  Renal tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome according to standard protocols. Histopathological changes were evaluated under light microscopy using a MoticEasyScan Pro 6 slide scanner (Motic Instruments, Hong Kong SAR, China). H&E-stained sections were used to assess renal tubular injury, whereas Masson’s trichrome-stained sections were used to evaluate renal interstitial fibrosis. All histological assessments were performed at ×100 magnification by an observer blinded to the experimental groups.
  Renal tubular injury and renal interstitial fibrosis were semi-quantitatively evaluated using a previously described scoring system with minor modifications [14]. Tubular injury was assessed based on vacuolar degeneration, tubular necrosis, tubular dilatation, and tubular atrophy, and was scored according to the percentage of affected tubules as follows: 0, normal; 1, <25%; 2, 25–49%; and 3, ≥50%. Renal interstitial fibrosis was assessed according to the extent of collagen deposition in the tubulointerstitial area on Masson’s trichrome-stained sections and was graded as follows: 0, no fibrosis; 1, <25%; 2, 25–49%; 3, 50–74%; and 4, ≥75%.

2.4 Evaluation of mitochondrial morphology by transmission electron microscopy
  Mitochondrial morphology was evaluated in ipsilateral obstructed renal tissues from UUO rats and the corresponding right renal tissues from sham-operated rats by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Briefly, ultrathin renal tissue sections from each experimental group were examined using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT-7700; Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The analysis focused on mitochondrial ultrastructural changes in renal tubular epithelial cells.
  Mitochondrial structural deformity was defined as deformation or loss of mitochondrial cristae and/or disruption of the inner mitochondrial membrane, as previously described [13]. For quantitative analysis, five randomly selected non-overlapping fields measuring 2 μm² each were analyzed per specimen, and the number of structurally deformed mitochondria was counted.




2.5 LC–MS/MS analysis
  Protein extracts (10 μg) obtained from ipsilateral obstructed renal tissues of UUO rats and the corresponding right renal tissues of sham-operated rats were subjected to in-solution digestion. Briefly, protein samples were diluted and digested with sequencing-grade modified porcine trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) at 37 °C for 16 h. The resulting peptides were desalted, dried by vacuum centrifugation, and stored at −80 °C until LC–MS/MS analysis.
  The tryptic peptides were reconstituted in high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) buffer A, consisting of 0.1% formic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and loaded onto a reversed-phase trap column (Zorbax 300SB-C18, 0.3 × 5 mm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Peptide separation was performed using an in-house-packed reversed-phase analytical column (HydroRP, 2.5 μm, 75 μm inner diameter × 20 cm, with a 15-μm tip) on a Dionex RSLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Peptides were separated over a 70-min multistep gradient using HPLC buffer B, consisting of 99.9% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid.
  The liquid chromatography system was coupled through an electrospray ionization interface to an Orbitrap Elite hybrid ion trap–Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated using Xcalibur 2.2 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MS analysis was performed in data-dependent acquisition mode. Each acquisition cycle consisted of one full MS scan followed by 20 MS/MS scans of the 20 most abundant precursor ions detected in the full MS scan. The automatic gain control target and maximum injection time were set to 3 × 10⁶ ions and 100 ms for full MS scans, and 3 × 10³ ions and 200 ms for MS/MS scans, respectively.

2.6 Protein identification and quantitation
  Protein identification and quantification were performed using Proteome Discoverer software version 2.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MS/MS spectra were searched against the SwissProt database restricted to Rattus norvegicus using the Mascot search engine version 2.5 (Matrix Science, London, UK). The database version, download date, and total number of sequences were recorded.
  For peptide identification, precursor and fragment ion mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm and 0.5 Da, respectively. Trypsin was selected as the digestion enzyme, and up to two missed cleavages were allowed. Only rank-one peptide-spectrum matches with high confidence were retained. The overall false discovery rate was controlled at <1%. Proteins identified by only a single peptide hit were excluded from further analysis.
  Relative protein abundance in renal tissues was estimated using label-free semi-quantitative analysis based on the spectral counting method [15]. For each sample, the normalized spectral count of each protein was calculated by dividing the spectral count of a given protein by the total spectral counts of that sample and then multiplying by the average total spectral count across all samples. The fold change in normalized spectral count was calculated by comparing the UUO group with the sham group.

2.7 Data analysis
  To evaluate the global proteomic profiles and physiological responses of renal tissues after UUO surgery, principal component analysis (PCA), correlation coefficient analysis, hierarchical clustering, and heat map visualization were performed using the Statistical Analysis module of MetaboAnalyst 5.0 [16]. The normalized spectral count data obtained from ipsilateral obstructed renal tissues of UUO rats and the corresponding right renal tissues of sham-operated rats were used for these analyses.
  PCA was performed to visualize the overall distribution and clustering pattern of proteomic profiles between the UUO and sham groups. Correlation coefficient analysis was conducted to assess the similarity of protein expression patterns among samples. For hierarchical clustering and heat map generation, Ward’s clustering algorithm was applied using Pearson’s distance as the similarity measure.

2.8 Functional enrichment analysis using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis 
  Differentially expressed proteins were defined as proteins showing a fold change ≥2.0 or ≤0.5 in the UUO group compared with the sham group. These proteins were subjected to functional enrichment analysis using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Qiagen Inc., Valencia, CA, USA).
  Canonical pathway enrichment was analyzed using the right-tailed Fisher’s exact test. The significance of pathway enrichment was expressed as −log10(P value), and pathways with −log10(P value) >1.3, corresponding to P < 0.05, were considered significantly enriched. The IPA activation z-score was used to indicate whether the enriched canonical pathways were predicted to be upregulated or downregulated. A positive z-score indicated predicted upregulation of the pathway, whereas a negative z-score indicated predicted downregulation.
  IPA toxicity function analysis was also performed using the Tox Lists module. Tox Lists consist of manually curated molecular sets associated with specific toxicological responses, including renal, hepatic, cardiac, and mitochondrial injury-related processes. In this study, Tox List analysis was used to identify toxicity-associated biological processes and mitochondrial dysfunction-related responses in ipsilateral obstructed renal tissues from UUO rats compared with the corresponding right renal tissues from sham-operated rats.

2.9 Western blotting assay
  To validate the expression of OXPHOS complex-related proteins identified by proteomic and IPA analyses, Western blotting was performed using ipsilateral obstructed renal tissues from UUO rats and the corresponding right renal tissues from sham-operated rats.
  Briefly, snap-frozen renal tissues were thawed and homogenized in protein extraction buffer. The homogenates were heated, centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for protein analysis. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce Chemical Co., Rockford, IL, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).
  After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the target proteins, including NDUFS1 (ab185733, 1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), SDHC (ab14714, 1:1000; Abcam), ATP5C1 (ab119686, 1:1000; Abcam), ATP5J (NBP2-15514, 1:1000; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA), and GAPDH (G9545, 1:5000; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The membranes were then incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution), such as anti-rabbit IgG (#NA934; Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Piscataway, NJ, USA) or anti-mouse IgG (#NA931; Amersham Pharmacia Biotech). Immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (ECL Plus kit; Amersham BioSciences, Buckinghamshire, UK).
  Chemiluminescent signals were captured using a UVP BioSpectrum 815 imaging system (Ultra-Violet Products Ltd., Upland, CA, USA), and band intensities were quantified using VisionWorks Life Science software (Ultra-Violet Products Ltd.). The expression levels of target proteins were normalized to GAPDH.

2.10 Mitochondrial complex I-V activity assays
  The activities of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I, II, III, IV, and V in the ipsilateral obstructed renal tissues and the corresponding right renal tissues from sham-operated rats were measured using specific assay kits according to the manufacturers' instructions. Briefly, renal tissues (100 mg) were washed with cold PBS and homogenized in extraction solution A containing protease inhibitors. The homogenates were centrifuged at 600×g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected and further centrifuged at 15,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the mitochondrial pellets. The pellets were resuspended in extraction solution B with protease inhibitors, subjected to sonication for 1 min, and centrifuged at 15,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were used for mitochondrial complex activity assays and protein quantification.
  The specific activities of complex I (E-BC-K149-M; Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA), complex II (E-BC-K150-M; Elabscience), complex III (E-BC-K151-M; Elabscience), complex IV (E-BC-K152-M; Elabscience), and complex V (E-BC-K153-M; Elabscience) were determined in kinetic mode using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x; Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA). Absorbance changes were monitored at specific wavelengths: 340 nm for Complex I and Complex V, 600 nm for Complex II, and 550 nm for Complex III and Complex IV. The reaction temperature was maintained at 37 °C. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce Chemical Co., Rockford, IL, USA) for normalization. The results are expressed as relative specific activities (U/mg protein) compared with the sham group.
