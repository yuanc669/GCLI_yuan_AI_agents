A novel thermo-sensitive polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based hydrogel formulation combining KCF18—a peptide
decoy that inhibits receptor binding of tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1β, and interleukin-6—was developed
to enable controlled release of KCF18 for the treatment of bladder fibrosis resulting from partial bladder outlet
obstruction (BOO). The hydrogel, composed of methyl PEG–poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (mPEG–PLGA, diblock
copolymer) and PLGA–PEG–PLGA (triblock copolymer) at a 5:5 wt ratio, was mixed with KCF18 (1.5 mg/kg) to
produce  a  20  %  KCF-hydrogel  solution  (100  μL).  Pharmacokinetic  analysis  revealed  a  peak  plasma  KCF18
concentration on Day 1, sustained through Day 2, with an estimated half-life of 3.5 days. In an adult male mouse
model  of  BOO-induced  bladder  fibrosis,  weekly  subcutaneous  administration  of  the  KCF-hydrogel  for  seven
weeks  improved  bladder  function,  reduced  fibrosis,  and  downregulated  transforming  growth  factor-β  and
fibrosis-related markers, including collagen I, vimentin, and α-smooth muscle actin. Therapeutic benefits were
observed when treatment was initiated during the early or mid-disease stages (Day 7 or 14 post-BOO), but not in
the late stage (Day 28). This thermo-sensitive PEG-based hydrogel enables controlled KCF18 release, offering a
promising therapeutic strategy for BOO-induced bladder fibrosis when administered in the early or mid-disease
stages.

1. Introduction

Benign  prostatic  enlargement  is  a  key  cause  of  bladder  outlet
obstruction  (BOO)  [1–4],  typically  managed  with  α-blockers  and
5α-reductase  inhibitors  [5].  However,  patient  adherence  to  these

treatments is often low [6], and without consistent intervention, BOO
can  lead  to  bladder  remodeling,  dysfunction,  and  fibrosis  [7].  This
progression causes urine retention, frequent urinary tract infections, and
renal function impairment [8]. When standard treatments are ineffec-
tive, surgery might be an option, but it cannot fully reverse advanced

bladder  fibrosis  [9–11].  Therefore,  developing  new  treatments  for
BOO-induced bladder fibrosis to prevent irreversible bladder damage is
crucial.

Increased  intravesical  pressure  during  urination  due  to  outlet
obstruction can cause ischemia-reperfusion injury in the bladder, lead-
ing  to  tissue  inflammation  [12,13].  Research  shows  that  bladder
inflammation  from  BOO  is  associated  with  elevated  levels  of  inflam-
matory  cytokines  like  tumor  necrosis  factor-α  (TNF-α),  interleukin-1β
(IL-1β), and IL-6 [14,15], which are crucial in starting bladder remod-
eling, dysfunction, and fibrosis [15,16]. This involves activating trans-
forming growth factor-β (TGF-β), a key regulator of fibrosis, in response
to  these  cytokines  [17].  Upon  TGF-β  activation,  a  complex  series  of
fibrotic  processes,  including  extracellular  matrix  (ECM)  remodeling
with excessive collagen production/deposition, epithelial-mesenchymal
transition  (EMT),  and  fibroblast  to  myofibroblast  transition  (FMT),
initiated  [18,19].  These  insights  highlight  TNF-α,  IL-1β,  and  IL-6  as
central  to  BOO-induced  bladder  fibrosis  and  potential  therapeutic
targets.

Peptide  decoys, engineered to target and  bind specific receptor  li-
gands,  exhibit  high-affinity  therapeutic  effect  and  have  shown  effec-
tiveness in treating disease like inflammatory diseases, cardiovascular
diseases and breast cancer [20–23]. KCF18, a designed peptide decoy
comprising 18 amino acids, incorporates N-terminal amino acids from
TNF receptor 1 (TNFR1), middle amino acids from IL-1 receptor (IL-1R),
and  C-terminal  amino  acids  from  IL-6  receptor  (IL-6R)  [21].  KCF18
effectively blocks TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 receptor bindings, mimicking
their  receptors’  binding  sites  [21].  Research  has  shown  that  KCF18
successfully  treats  lung  and  liver  injuries  in  mice  by  blocking  TNF-α,
IL-1β,  and  IL-6  receptor  bindings  [23],  suggesting  its  potential  effec-
tiveness in treating BOO-induced bladder fibrosis.

Peptides’  short  in  vivo  half-life,  due  to  rapid  degradation  and
clearance, limits their use in chronic diseases like BOO-induced bladder
fibrosis  [24].  However,  integrating  them  with  polyethylene  glycol
(PEG)-based  hydrogels  can  enhance  their  stability  and  lifespan  [25].
These  hydrogels  can  encapsulate  peptides  within  a  three-dimensional
network,  effectively  restricting  their  diffusion  and  protecting  them
from  rapid  degradation,  thereby  improving  in  vivo  retention  and  cir-
culation time, while also enabling controlled release like the extracel-
lular  matrix  [25,26].  For  instance,  a  thermo-sensitive  PEG-hydrogel
system has been shown to extend monoclonal antibody release for up to
21 days [27]. Thermosensitive PEG-based hydrogels, with their inject-
ability, biocompatibility, and physiological sol–gel transition behavior,
are  increasingly  applied  in  therapy.  Functional  modifications  further
enhance their utility in disease models including cancer, fibrosis, and
inflammation [28–32], supporting their use in the development of our
KCF18-loaded hydrogel system.

In  our  study  targeting  BOO-induced  bladder  fibrosis,  we  tested  a
thermo-sensitive  PEG-based  hydrogel  for  the  controlled  release  of
KCF18  (KCF-hydrogel),  hypothesizing  continuous  cytokine  inhibition
using KCF-hydrogel can alleviate bladder issues in a proven murine BOO
model. We also examined the intervention’s effects on related biological
pathways. Additionally, this study also evaluated the impact of disease
progression  on  the  therapeutic  efficacy  of  KCF18-hydrogel  in  this
context.

2.3. Bladder fibrosis progression in BOO mice

2.3.1. The surgical procedures of BOO

The protocols for the BOO surgical procedures were adapted from a
previous report [36]. With anesthesia (zoletil/xylazine, 40/10 mg/kg,
intraperitoneal  injection),  mice  received  a  0.5-cm  midline  vertical
incision, extending from the penoscrotal junction to the mid-scrotum, to
access  the  bulbous  urethra.  Following  isolation  from  the  cavernous
bodies, the urethral surface was exposed, and a sterile metal bar with a
0.3-mm diameter was positioned. A 4-0 vicryl suture (J504G; Ethicon
Inc., Bridgewater, NJ, USA) was used to create a tie around both the
urethra and the metal bar. Subsequently, the metal bar was removed,
leaving  the  urethra  partially  obstructed.  The  muscle  layer  was
re-approximated, and the skin closed using a 3-0 nylon suture (663G;
Ethicon Inc.). The incision wound was cleansed with a 10 % povidone
iodine  solution  (10  %  w/v;  Betadine®.  Win-Medicare  Pvt  Ltd.,  New
Dehli, India), and immediate postoperative analgesia was administered
with local injection of bupivacaine (0.5 %, 100 μL; Pfizer, New York, NY,
USA).  The  mice  were  placed  in  cages  and  maintained  with  a  heating
lamp.  As  a  control  for  the  surgical  procedures,  a  separate  group  of
anesthetized mice underwent a sham operation, involving a penoscrotal
incision and urethra isolation without the placement of a urethral tie.

2.3.2. BOO-induced bladder function deterioration measured using mice
voiding behavior analysis

To confirm the development and progression of bladder fibrosis in
this BOO murine model, a set of 15 mice received BOO surgery and 5
mice  received  sham  operation.  On  days  7,  14,  and  28  post-BOO,  the
bladder function of the BOO mice (5 mice per time point) was evaluated
using voiding behavior analysis (designated as the BOO-7D, BOO-14D
and  BOO-28D  groups,  respectively), as  per  established  methods  [37].
Voiding behavior analysis was also conducted in sham-operated mice
(Sham group). Briefly, on the day of evaluation, each mouse was indi-
vidually housed in a cage furnished with standard rodent chow and lined
with  a  0.35-mm  filter  paper  (26.5  × 21  cm;  No.  GFP001;  GenePure,
Taichung, Taiwan) for a duration of 4 h in a dark room. Urine spots on
the  filter  papers  were  captured  using  a  documentation  system  (UVP
UVsolo  touch  darkroom  ultraviolet  imaging  cabinet;  Analytik,  Jena,
Germany). Image capture settings were adjusted using the UVP Vision-
Works touch image acquisition software (Jena). Images were obtained
using  the  ninhydrin  test  (1  %  ninhydrin  in  75  %  ethanol  solution;
Sigma-Aldrich)  and  365-nm  UV  illumination.  The  exposure  settings
were optimized to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, following which the
counts  of  urine  spots  (urinary  frequency)  and  areas  of  urine  spots
(bladder capacity) were recorded.

2.4. Therapeutic effects of KCF-hydrogel against bladder fibrosis in BOO
mice

To investigate the therapeutic effects of KCF-hydrogel, a treatment
regimen  involving  weekly  subcutaneous  injections  of  KCF-hydrogel
(100 μL; 20 %, 5:5), containing 1.5 mg/kg KCF18, at the back of the
neck for 7 consecutive weeks was designed.

The KCF18 dosage used in this study, as previously mentioned, was
initially determined based on our earlier findings that 1.5 mg/kg KCF18
effectively inhibits the binding of TNF-α/TNFR, IL-1β/IL-1R, and IL-6/
IL-6R  [23].  Additionally,  we  conducted  a  preliminary  study  to  eval-
uate  the  therapeutic  effects  of  KCF-hydrogel  containing  KCF18  at  a
lower dose (0.6 mg/kg). The results showed that the 0.6 mg/kg dose did
not produce significant improvements in bladder dysfunction or fibrosis
(Supplemental Fig. 1C). Based on these findings, the 1.5 mg/kg dose was
selected for further investigation in this study.

In addition, our data showed that KCF-hydrogel facilitated the sus-
tained  release  of  KCF18  for  up  to  7  days  (please  refer  to  Fig.  3).
Furthermore, we observed significant bladder dysfunction and bladder
wall  fibrosis  by  Day  7  following  BOO  induction  (early  stage),  with
worsening symptoms by Days 14 and 28 (mid- and late disease stages)
(please refer to Figs. 5 and 6). Based on these observations, the thera-

3.1. Characteristics of the KCF-hydrogel

Fig.  1 shows  the  in  vitro  release  profile  of  the  four  KCF-hydrogel
formulations.  All  formulations  exhibited  a  similar  pattern,  character-
ized by a rapid increase in release within the first 8 h, followed by a
slower increase through Days 1–2. Notably, the 20 % KCF-hydrogel (5:5)
formulation  demonstrated  the  highest  in  vitro  release  efficacy,  with
approximately 55 % released by Day 2. In comparison, the other three
formulations released approximately 30–35 % by 8 h and around 35–45
%  by  Day  2.  Based  on  these  results,  the  20  %  KCF-hydrogel  (5:5)
formulation was selected for subsequent experiments.

Fig. 2 presents the structural characteristics of the KCF-hydrogel (20
%, 5:5) and its corresponding control hydrogel, as observed by scanning
electron microscopy. Fig. 3 illustrates the physicochemical properties of
both  hydrogels,  including  (A)  phase  diagram  analysis,  (B)  shrinkage
behavior,  (C)  representative  photographs  of  both  hydrogels  using  the
tube inversion method, and (D) rheological measurements. The results
indicate that incorporation of the KCF18 peptide did not significantly
affect  these  parameters,  confirming  that  the  fundamental  physico-
chemical characteristics of the hydrogel were preserved.

Fig. 4 shows the plasma concentrations of KCF18, which peaked at
approximately  24  h  (Day  1),  plateaued  until  48  h  (Day  2),  and  then
gradually declined, with KCF18 remaining detectable up to 144 h (Day
6). Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed a maximum plasma concentration
(Cmax) of 20.53 ng/mL, with the time to reach maximum concentration
(Tmax) occurring at 48 h. The estimated in vivo half-life was approxi-
mately  84  h  (3.5  days),  the  area  under  the  concentration–time  curve
(AUC) was 1805.55 ng h/mL, and the mean residence time (MRT) was
58 h.

Fig. 5 shows KCF18 biodistribution in mice, with fluorescence signals
indicating its presence in organs. Increased fluorescence was observed in
the liver, kidney, and bladder, particularly the kidney, while the heart,
lungs, and spleen had low signals. Quantitative analysis showed signif-
icant fluorescence in the kidney on Days 1, 3, and 5 (p = 0.022, p =
0.006 and p = 0.002, respectively), in the liver on Day 1 (p = 0.006), and
in  the bladder on  Day 3 (p  = 0.037).  No significant  differences  were
found  in  the  heart,  lungs  or  spleen.  Moreover,  IVIS  imaging  of  mice
treated with rhodamine B–only hydrogel showed minimal and transient
fluorescence (please refer to Supplemental Fig. 1A). These data confirm
that the fluorescent signals were emitted by rhodamine B-KCF, but not
rhodamine B hydrolyzed from the conjugate.

4. Discussion

The data from this study demonstrate that the thermo-sensitive PEG-
based  hydrogels  facilitate  the  controlled  release  of  KCF18,  a  peptide
decoy that inhibits TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 receptor binding [21]. Among
the four formulations tested, the 20 % KCF-hydrogel (5:5) demonstrated
the best KCF18 release profile, outperforming the 25 % (5:5), 25 % (4:6),

and 20 % (4:6) formulations. The study also shows that BOO induces
significant bladder fibrosis and upregulation of inflammatory cytokines
(TNF-α,  IL-1β,  and  IL-6),  TGF-β  (a  master  regulator  of  fibrosis),  and
markers of fibrotic processes, including collagen I (for ECM remodeling),
α-SMA (for FMT), and vimentin (for EMT) [18,19]. These effects become
apparent by Day 7 (early stage) and worsen by Days 14 and 28 (mid- and
late stages). Furthermore, the data highlight that continuous inhibition
of TNF-α, IL-1β  and IL-6 receptor binding, mediated by the controlled
release of KCF18 from the thermo-sensitive PEG-based hydrogel (20 %,
5:5),  can  mitigate  bladder  fibrosis  and  suppress  the  upregulation  of
TGF-β  and  fibrosis  markers  induced  by  BOO  in  mice.  These  findings
support  our  hypothesis  and  emphasize  the  potential  of  targeting  the
upregulation  of  inflammatory  cytokines  as  a  therapeutic  strategy  for
BOO-induced  bladder  fibrosis.  Notably,  therapeutic  benefits  are
observed when treatment is initiated during the early or mid-stages of
BOO, but not during the late stage. These results further underline the
importance of timely intervention in this context. Given the current lack
of effective therapies to inhibit BOO-induced bladder fibrosis, the data

As  highlighted  above,  timely  intervention  is  critical  in  managing
BOO-induced  bladder  fibrosis.  Our  study  demonstrates  that  disease
progression significantly impacts the therapeutic efficacy of continuous
cytokine  inhibition,  which  works  by  blocking  cytokine-receptor  bind-
ings.  While  cytokine  inhibition  mitigates  bladder  dysfunction  and
fibrosis in the early and mid-stages of BOO-induced bladder fibrosis, its
effects are less pronounced in the late stage. One possible explanation
for this is that, once TGF-β  expression and bladder fibrosis are firmly
established in the late stage, a vicious cycle of heightened intravesical
pressure during urination and continuous upregulation of cytokines due
to bladder fibrosis is set in motion. As a result, inhibiting cytokines from
binding to their receptors may no longer effectively reduce inflamma-
tory response as well as decrease TGF-β levels and modulate its activity
in  the  late  stage  of  BOO-induced  bladder  fibrosis.  This  hypothesis  is
supported  by  our  data  from  the  late  stage  of  BOO-induced  bladder
fibrosis,  where  KCF-hydrogel  treatment  showed  limited  effects  on
modulating cytokine upregulation, upregulation of fibrosis markers and
bladder fibrosis. Based on these findings, we conjecture that therapies
targeting TGF-β directly—either by inhibiting its expression or blocking
its  interaction  with  the  TGF-β  receptor—may  provide  greater  thera-
peutic  benefits  in  the  later  stages  of  BOO-induced  bladder  fibrosis.
Currently, our laboratory is conducting follow-up studies to explore the
potential of novel peptide decoys that can block TGF-β receptor binding
in this context.

Our  study  has  several  inherent  limitations.  Firstly,  the  bio-
distribution of KCF18 in bladder tissues post-injection was suboptimal,
suggesting that adjustments in dosing or administration techniques are
needed to improve local delivery to the bladder. Moreover, a discrep-
ancy  between  the  fluorescence  biodistribution  and  pharmacokinetic

profiles was observed, as KCF18 accumulation in the bladder peaked on
Day 3, while plasma concentrations peaked between 24 and 48 h (Days
1–2).  Although  the  underlying  mechanisms  remain  unclear,  this
discrepancy  may  reflect  differences  in  biological  compartments  and
detection  methods:  plasma  pharmacokinetics  represent  systemic  drug
levels,  whereas  IVIS  imaging  reflects  local  tissue  accumulation.
Following subcutaneous hydrogel administration, the drug is gradually
released into circulation and subsequently distributed to tissues, making
it plausible for plasma levels to peak earlier than tissue signals observed
by IVIS. Further studies are needed to clarify this mechanism. Secondly,
our  preliminary  data  demonstrated  that  the  KCF-hydrogel  containing
KCF18 at 1.5 mg/kg, but not at 0.6 mg/kg (please refer to Supplemental
Fig. 1C), significantly attenuated BOO-induced bladder dysfunction and
fibrosis. Furthermore, serum biochemistry analyses revealed no signif-
icant  liver  or  kidney  toxicity  in  mice  treated  with  the  KCF-hydrogel
containing  KCF18  1.5  mg/kg,  supporting  its  in  vivo  biocompatibility
(please refer to Supplemental Fig. 1D). Nevertheless, it remains to be
determined  whether  doses  higher  than  1.5  mg/kg  could  confer  addi-
tional  therapeutic  benefits  in  this  context,  and  potential  toxicity  at
higher  doses  also  remains  unstudied.  Thirdly,  among  the  four  KCF-
hydrogel  formulations  tested,  the  20  %  KCF-hydrogel  (5:5)  demon-
strated  the  most  favorable  in  vitro  release  efficacy.  Based  on  this
comparative  release  profile,  we  selected  this  formulation  for  further
studies to ensure more efficient drug delivery within the critical early
treatment window. While we acknowledge that longer release durations
may be desirable in certain contexts, the observed performance of this
formulation provided a practical balance between release efficacy and
experimental  feasibility.  At  this  stage,  our  data  did  not  indicate  a
definitive “incomplete release” issue with the other formulations; rather,
the selection was guided by relative release performance. Nonetheless,
the  underlying  mechanisms contributing  to the  superior  release  char-
acteristics  of  the  20  %  KCF-hydrogel  (5:5)  remain  unclear.  It  is  also
uncertain  whether  alternative  hydrogel  formulations—such  as  those
with modified polymer compositions or concentrations—could further
enhance  controlled  release,  highlighting  the  need  for  additional
investigation.

Fourthly,  previous  studies  have  demonstrated  the  efficacy  of  anti-
inflammatory  therapies  (e.g.,  metformin,  mesenchymal  stem  cells,
etc.) [41,42] in treating bladder fibrosis. In a BOO mouse model, met-
formin  administered  daily  for  9  weeks  restored  bladder  voiding  effi-
ciency to approximately 70 % of normal levels [41]. In comparison, our
KCF-hydrogel  therapy,  administered  weekly  for  7  weeks,  restored
voiding  efficiency  to  86  %.  In  terms  of  anti-fibrotic  effects,  human
adipose-derived  stem  cell  therapy  administered  for  4  weeks  reduced
TGF-β  expression  to  approximately  65  %  [42],  whereas  our
KCF-hydrogel  therapy  reduced  TGF-β  levels  to  around  33  %  after  7
weeks of treatment. Although no direct head-to-head comparisons were
conducted,  these  findings  suggest  that  KCF-hydrogel  therapy  demon-
strates robust therapeutic efficacy with a potentially more convenient
dosing schedule and translational feasibility. Furthermore, monoclonal
antibodies  [43–45]  or  fusion  proteins  such  as  TAL-6  [46]  targeting
TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 represent another approach to cytokine inhibi-
tion. However,  this study did not directly compare KCF18 with  these
treatments, raising the question of whether KCF-hydrogel may outper-
form them. Nevertheless, KCF18 offers the advantage of simultaneous
inhibition of all three cytokines, potentially simplifying treatment and
reducing  costs.  The  data  from  this  study  thus  lay  the  foundation  for
future research and clinical applications, offering hope for patients and
underscoring  the  need  for  continued  investigations.  Lastly,  bladder
fibrosis can also occur in females, such as in cases of interstitial cystitis
[47].  Whether  KCF-hydrogel  therapy  can  provide  similar  therapeutic
benefits in this context remains to be explored.

In  conclusion,  our  study  demonstrates  that  partial  bladder  outlet
obstruction drives bladder inflammation and fibrosis. Timely interven-
tion  with  sustained  cytokine  inhibition  in  the  early  and  mid-disease
stages,  enabled  by  thermo-sensitive  PEG-based  hydrogel-mediated
controlled  KCF18  peptide  decoy  release,  effectively  mitigates  inflam-
mation and reduces bladder fibrosis in male mice.
