We investigated eﬀects of tyrosol on upstream pathway regulating lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced in-
ﬂammatory response, including cluster of diﬀerentiation 14 (CD14)-mediated LPS–macrophage binding, toll-like
receptor 4 (TLR4)/myeloid diﬀerentiation factor 2 (MD2) expression and subsequent myeloid diﬀerentiation
primary response gene 88 (MyD88) and TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF) recruit-
ments. Flow cytometry revealed that, compared with LPS-treated RAW264.7 cells (LPS group), those treated
with LPS and tyrosol (1.2 mM) [LPS+T(1.2) group] demonstrated lower LPS–macrophage binding and mem-
brane-bound CD14 (mCD14) and TLR4/MD2 expression (all p < 0.05). Immunoprecipitation/immunoblotting
assay revealed lower MyD88 and TRIF concentrations in the LPS+T(1.2) group than in the LPS group (both
p < 0.05). Soluble CD14 concentration was higher in the LPS+T(1.2) group than in the LPS group (p = 0.013);
protease inhibition counteracted this eﬀect. Thus, tyrosol inhibits LPS–macrophage binding, mCD14 and TLR4/
MD2 expression, and MyD88 and TRIF recruitment, all possibly by enhancing protease-mediated mCD14 clea-
vage.

1. Introduction

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), also called the gram-negative endotoxin,
is a major component of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria
that can activate major inﬂammatory signalling pathways in macro-
phages (Manthey & Vogel, 1994). The pathways begin with LPS–ma-
crophage binding mediated by the accessory proteins, namely the cir-
culating LPS-binding protein (LBP) and the cell surface pattern
recognition receptor cluster of diﬀerentiation 14 (CD14) (Park & Lee,
2013; Schumann, 1992). LBP is generally believed to present LPS to

CD14, which then facilitates the chaperone of monomeric forms of LPS
and enhances LPS transfer to the toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/myeloid
diﬀerentiation factor 2 (MD2) complex (Fitzgerald, Rowe, & Golenbock,
2004; Jerala, 2007; Pålsson-McDermott and O'Neill, 2004; Park & Lee,
2013; Schumann, 1992). CD14 exists in two forms: membrane-bound
CD14 (mCD14), which anchors to the membrane by a glycosylpho-
sphatidylinositol tail,
(Kirkland &
Viriyakosol, 1998), produced through either the proteolytic cleavage of
mCD14 (i.e., shedding of CD14) or direct secretion from intracellular
vesicles (Pham, 2008; Tada et al., 2002). The eﬀects of mCD14 and

sCD14 are complex. mCD14 is responsible for exposure of LPS to TLR4/
MD2 (Berenger et al., 2011). However, sCD14 may suppress responses
to LPS by removing LPS from mCD14 (Berenger et al., 2011). The
crucial role of CD14 has been highlighted previously: CD14 is an es-
sential factor enabling the LPS receptor to switch into the “full activa-
tion” mode (Jiang et al., 2005).

The TLR4/MD2 complex comprises an extracellular adaptor protein
MD2 and a transmembrane receptor TLR4 (Park & Lee, 2013;
Schumann, 1992). The binding of LPS to TLR4/MD2 induces TLR4/
MD2 activation, including TLR4/MD2 dimerization and subsequent
TLR4 endocytosis (Fitzgerald et al., 2004; Jerala, 2007; Pålsson-
McDermott and O'Neill, 2004; Park & Lee, 2013; Schumann, 1992).
Dimerization of TLR4/MD2 at the cell surface induces recruitment of
myeloid diﬀerentiation primary response gene 88 (MyD88) (Kagan &
Medzhitov, 2006). TLR4 endocytosis into endosomes then induces re-
cruitment of TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β
(TRIF) (O'Neill et al., 2003; Yamamoto et al., 2003). The MyD88-
mediated early response then activates transcription factors nuclear
factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) and
subsequently induces upregulation of inﬂammatory molecules (e.g.,
cytokines) (Christman, Lancaster, & Blackwell, 1998; Kristof, Marks-
Konczalik, & Moss, 2001). The subsequent TRIF-mediated response
activates interferon regulatory factor 3 and then induces interferon-β
production followed by NF-κB activation (Kagan et al., 2008).

Tyrosol [2-(4-hydroxyphenyl)ethanol] is a polyphenol presents in
natural sources, including olive oil, red wine, and white wine (Covas
et al., 2003; Miró-Casas et al., 2001). Tyrosol has potent antioxidant
and anti-inﬂammatory properties (Sato et al., 2016; Serra, Deiana,
Spencer, & Corona, 2017). It can inhibit LPS-induced cytokine pro-
duction in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (Giovannini
et al., 2002) and mitigate inﬂammatory response, inhibit organ injuries,
and increase survival in rodents with endotoxemia (Kim et al., 2017; Lu
et al., 2013). Moreover, the involvement of NF-κB and MAPKs mediate
the anti-inﬂammation eﬀects of tyrosol (Sato et al., 2016; Wang et al.,
2017). These data highlight the eﬀectiveness of tyrosol in modulating
LPS signal transduction pathways. CD14, as mentioned, plays a crucial
role in mediating LPS-induced TLR4/MD2 activation. However, whe-
ther tyrosol modulates the aforementioned upstream pathways remains
unclear. To further elucidate this, we conducted a cellular study by
hypothesising that tyrosol inhibits LPS–macrophage binding, mitigates
mCD14 activation, increases sCD14 expression, inhibits TLR4/MD2
expression, and reduces MyD88 and TRIF recruitment in LPS-treated
macrophages. With previous data supporting the beneﬁcial eﬀects of
tyrosol, the current data can provide detailed information regarding the
upstream regulatory pathways involved in mediating the anti-in-
ﬂammatory eﬀects of tyrosol. Findings from this study will help ad-
vance knowledge in this area.

In summary, this study investigated the eﬀects of tyrosol on mod-
ulating the upstream pathways regulating LPS-induced inﬂammatory
response involving CD14, TLR4/MD2, MyD88, and TRIF.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Cell culture and activation with LPS, tyrosol administration, and cell
viability

RAW264.7 cell line, a widely used immortalised murine macro-
phage-like cell line, was grown and incubated as per our previously
published protocol (Huang, Tsai, Kai, Yang, & Huang, 2006). Only the
cells that had undergone < 20 passages were used in this experiment.
The cells were then treated with 100 ng/mL LPS (Escherichia coli
Serotype 0127:B8 endotoxin; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to
induce expression of the molecules under investigation. LPS dose was
determined according to our previous report (Chen, Tsai, & Huang,
2012). For LPS–macrophage binding assay through ﬂow cytometry,
another set of RW264.7 cells was treated with LPS labelled with

ﬂuorescein isothiocyanate (FITC-LPS; 1000 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich).
FITC-LPS dose was selected according to our preliminary data, such that
the ﬂuorescence signal was detectable. Our preliminary data also re-
vealed that the interleukin 6 (IL-6) concentration in RAW264.7 cells
treated with 1000 ng/mL LPS-FITC was approximately 80% of that in
cells treated with 1000 ng/mL LPS and approximately threefold higher
than that in cells treated with 100 ng/mL LPS. Tyrosol (0.3, 0.6, or
1.2 mM; 98% purity, 188825, Sigma-Aldrich) was administered to
RAW264.7 cells 60 min before LPS. The administration time and doses
of tyrosol were determined according to previous reports (Kim et al.,
2017; Wang et al., 2017). Tyrosol was present in the LPS incubation
time in RAW264.7 cells treated with both LPS and tyrosol.

The cell viability of each group was assessed through ﬂuorometric
detection of living cell numbers using a commercial kit (Cell Counting
Kit-8, CCK-8; Sigma-Aldrich). In brief, cells were seeded in 96-well
plates (2 × 104 cells per well; Corning Life Sciences, Oneonta, NY, USA)
and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Then, RAW264.7 cells
were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS alone (LPS group) or in combination
with 0.3, 0.6, or 1.2 mM tyrosol [Sigma-Aldrich; denoted as LPS
+T(0.3), LPS+T(0.6), and LPS+T(1.2) groups, respectively]. LPS and
tyrosol were administered as above-mentioned. In addition, another
two groups of RAW264.7 cells were treated with phosphate-buﬀered
saline (PBS; Sigma-Aldrich) and tyrosol (1.2 mM) to serve as the vehicle
and tyrosol controls, respectively. These two groups were denoted as
the PBS and T(1.2) groups, respectively. Tyrosol was dissolved in 5 μL
of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich); thus, 5 μL of DMSO was
also added in the cells not receiving tyrosol. After 18 h of LPS admin-
istration or at the comparable time point in groups without LPS ad-
ministration, the culture medium of each well was removed. Then,
10 µL CCK-8 solution (Sigma-Aldrich) in 100 µL fresh culture medium
was added per well and the plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C with
5% CO2. Then, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a mi-
croplate reader, as per the manufacturer’s protocol.

2.2. Measuring IL-6 secretion by using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

○

To determine the eﬀects of tyrosol on IL-6 production, RAW 264.7
cells were seeded in 6-well plates (5 × 105 cells per well; Corning) and
incubated at 37
C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. The cells were then treated
with PBS, 1.2 mM tyrosol, 100 ng/mL LPS alone, 100 ng/mL LPS in

LPS, LPS+T(0.3), LPS+T(0.6), and LPS+T(1.2) groups, respectively].
LPS and tyrosol were administered as mentioned in Section 2.1. After

each group was then assayed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent
assay (ELISA). A commercial IL-6 ELISA kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Inc.,
Rockfold, IL, USA) was used, and analyses were performed as per the
manufacturer’s protocol.

the nuclear and cytosolic extracts of the cell cultures were prepared

MAPK activation is evident by phosphorylation of MAPKs (Kristof et al.,
2001). For MAPKs phosphorylation assay, the membranes of cytosolic
extracts were incubated with primary antibody of phosphorylated ex-

plus kit; Amersham). Densitometry technology (ImageJ, freely available
at http://rsb.info.nih.gov/ij/) was used to quantify densities of the
protein bands.

2.8. Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance and post hoc pairwise comparisons
with Tukey’s test were applied to determine the diﬀerences between the
groups. A p of < 0.05 was considered signiﬁcant. SPSS (version 16.0 for
Windows; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for statistical analyses.

3. Results

3.1. Tyrosol inhibited LPS-induced IL-6 secretion

Fig. 1A illustrated the data of cell viability (n = 5 in each group).
The mean CCK-8 absorption of each group were compared with the
mean CCK-8 absorption in the PBS group and expressed as the relative
absorption. Our data revealed comparable cell viabilities in the PBS,
T(1.2), LPS, LPS+T(0.3), LPS+T(0.6), and LPS+T(1.2) groups (all
p > 0.05).

Fig. 1B illustrated the data of IL-6 (n = 5 in each group). Our data
revealed that the PBS and T(1.2) groups had low IL-6 concentrations.
The IL-6 concentration was signiﬁcantly higher in the LPS group than in
the PBS group (p < 0.001). IL-6 concentrations did not diﬀer sig-
niﬁcantly between the LPS+T(0.3) and LPS group, whereas IL-6 con-
centrations were signiﬁcantly lower in the LPS+T(0.6) and LPS
+T(1.2) groups than in the LPS group in a dose-dependent manner (all
p < 0.05).

3.2. Tyrosol inhibited LPS-induced NF-κB and MAPKs expression

Fig. 2 illustrated the representative gel photography and densito-
metry analyses data of NF-κB expression (n = 4 in each group). Our
data revealed that the nuclear concentrations of p-NF-κB (Fig. 2A) and
the cytosolic concentrations of p-IκB (Fig. 2B) in the PBS and T(1.2)
groups were low. In contrast, the nuclear concentration of p-NF-κB and
the cytosolic concentration of p-IκB in the LPS group were signiﬁcantly
higher than in the PBS group (both p < 0.001). Of note, the nuclear
concentration of p-NF-κB and the cytosolic concentration of p-IκB in the
LPS+T(1.2) group were signiﬁcantly lower than in the LPS group
(p < 0.001 and = 0.003, respectively).

Fig. 3 illustrated the representative gel photography (Fig. 3A) and
densitometry analyses data (Fig. 3B–D) of MAPKs expression (n = 4 in
each group). Our data revealed that the concentrations of p-ERK
(Fig. 3B), p-JNK (Fig. 3C) and p-p38 MAPK (Fig. 3D) in the PBS and
T(1.2) groups were low. As expected, the concentrations of p-ERK, p-
JNK and p-p38 MAPK in the LPS group were signiﬁcantly higher than in
the PBS group (all p < 0.001). In contrast, the concentrations of p-
ERK, p-JNK and p-p38 MAPK in the LPS+T(1.2) group were sig-
niﬁcantly lower than in the LPS group (p = 0.027, 0.020 and 0.007,
respectively).

3.3. Tyrosol inhibited LPS binding to RAW264.7 cells

LPS binding to RAW264.7 cells was measured at 0 and 15 min after

4. Discussion

Studies have highlighted the potent anti-inﬂammatory capacity of
tyrosol (Giovannini et al., 2002; Kim et al., 2017; Lu et al., 2013; Sato
et al., 2016; Serra et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017). Findings from this
study demonstrated that tyrosol can inhibit inﬂammatory cytokine IL-6
upregulation in a dose-dependent manner. These data are consistent
with previous data that tyrosol can inhibit LPS-induced cytokine pro-
duction in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (Giovannini
et al., 2002). In addition, the eﬀects of tyrosol on mitigating NF-κB and
MAPKs upregulation were also observed by this study. These data
provide clear evidence to support the concept that NF-κB and MAPKs
are involved in mediating the anti-inﬂammation eﬀects of tyrosol (Sato
et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2017). This study further elucidated the eﬀects
of tyrosol on modulating the upstream pathway regulating LPS-induced

inﬂammatory signalling pathways. In activated macrophages, this study
conﬁrmed that tyrosol (1) inhibits LPS–macrophage binding, (2) miti-
gates mCD14 activation by enhancing CD14 shedding, (3) mitigates
TLR4/MD2 expression, and (4) reduces MyD88 and TRIF recruitments.
This is the ﬁrst evidence highlighting the anti-inﬂammatory eﬀects of
tyrosol involving its mediation in the upstream regulatory pathways.
The current ﬁndings contribute to the knowledge regarding tyrosol and
can potentially expand the scope of this natural polyphenol’s applica-
tion.

As noted in previous study, LPS–macrophage binding is mediated by
CD14 (Jerala, 2007; Schumann, 1992). Moreover, the activation of the
TLR4/MD2 complex and subsequent recruitment of MyD88 and TRIF is
also mediated by CD14 (Fitzgerald et al., 2004; Jerala, 2007; Pålsson-
McDermott and O'Neill, 2004; Park & Lee, 2013; Schumann, 1992).
This study highlighted the central role of CD14 in mediating the LPS-
induced inﬂammatory signalling pathways; notably, tyrosol enhances
CD14 shedding and subsequently mitigates mCD14 upregulation.
Therefore, in LPS-activated macrophages, tyrosol likely acts by en-
hancing CD14 shedding to inhibit CD14-mediated LPS–macrophage

TLR4 alone cannot recognise LPS (Maeshima & Fernandez, 2013;
Shimazu et al., 1999). By contrast, MD2 consists of one large open-
binding hydrophobic pocket, which can accommodate LPS (Shimazu
et al., 1999). Therefore, forming a stable 1:1 complex with MD2 (i.e.,
the TLR4/MD2 complex) is essential for TLR4 to recognise LPS. On
contact with LPS transferred by CD14, the hydrophobic pocket of MD2
can accommodate up to ﬁve fatty acid chains of LPS (Billod, Lacetera,
Guzmán-Caldentey, & Martín-Santamaría, 2016). The sixth fatty acid
chain of LPS protrudes from MD2 pocket and forms a TLR4/MD2 di-
merization interface (Billod et al., 2016). Based on its eﬀects on
transferring LPS to MD2, CD14 is essential in mediating TLR4/MD2
dimerization. In addition, TLR4 is highly dynamic and thus it moves
freely in the plasma membrane (Klein et al., 2015). In the presence of
LPS, an immobilised fraction of TLR4 in the plasma membrane forms
(Klein et al., 2015). This immobilised fraction appears in small punctate
structures in the plasma membrane containing CD14/LPS (Klein et al.,
2015). Forming the punctuate structures in the plasma membrane is
believed to prepare TLR4 for endocytosis (Klein et al., 2015). The
presence of CD14/LPS in these structures highlights the crucial roles of
CD14 in mediating TLR4 endocytosis. As ﬁndings from this study de-
monstrated the eﬀects of tyrosol on inhibiting both CD14 upregulation
and TLR4/MD2 expression, we thus believe that tyrosol may very likely
act by inhibiting CD14 to exert its eﬀects on mitigating TLR4/MD2
expression.

The potent anti-inﬂammatory capacity of tyrosol is conﬁrmed by
this study. These in vitro data support the concept that tyrosol has
therapeutic potential for inﬂammatory disease. The main source of
tyrosol is the Mediterranean diet (Miró-Casas et al., 2001). Epidemio-
logical studies showed polyphenols in the Mediterranean diet reduce
the risk of coronary heart disease mortality and morbidity (Miró-Casas
et al., 2001). Bioavailability of tyrosol is the main issue for applying the
ﬁndings from bench to bedside. Tyrosol is absorbed in a dose-depen-
dent manner with intake of moderate doses of phenol-rich natural
sources (e.g., olive oil) (Covas et al., 2003; Miró-Casas et al., 2001).
Tyrosol is relatively stable in gastro-intestinal transit and mainly ab-
sorbed in small intestine (Vissers, Zock, Roodenburg, Leenen, & Katan,
2002). Only a fraction (approximate 20–22%) of tyrosol is excreted in
urine within 24 h after ingestion (Covas et al., 2003; Miró-Casas et al.,
2001; Vissers et al., 2002; Visioli et al., 2000). Collectively, these data
indicate that tyrosol from phenol-rich natural sources can be absorbed
and used eﬀectively by the human body (Covas et al., 2003; Miró-Casas
et al., 2001; Vissers et al., 2002; Visioli et al., 2000). In vivo data de-
rived from rats further demonstrated a very high oral bioavailability of
tyrosol (up to 98%) (Tuck, Freeman, Hayball, Stretch, & Stupans,
2001). Natural sources of tyrosol, as mentioned, include olive oil, red
wine, and white wine (Covas et al., 2003; Miró-Casas et al., 2001).

diﬀerent when administered as aqueous and oil solutions (Lyons,
Charman, Miller, & Porter, 2000). This concept is conﬁrmed by pre-

from 98% to 75% if tyrosol is orally administered in an aqueous solu-

endotoxin-induced inﬂammatory response. The data indicated the
therapeutic potential of tyrosol against clinical conditions related to
endotoxin-induced inﬂammation (e.g., sepsis). This concept is sup-

induced organ injuries and increase survival
in rodents with en-

therapeutic eﬀects and serve as a therapeutic adjuvant against sepsis; it
may also mitigate organ injuries and improve outcomes in patients with
endotoxin-induced sepsis. Additional clinical studies are required be-
fore our ﬁndings translate into clinical use. In addition, our results seem
to conﬁrm that regular intake of olive oil (as in a typical Mediterranean
diet) may provide beneﬁcial eﬀects and boost anti-inﬂammatory ca-
pacity against conditions related to endotoxin-induced inﬂammatory
response. These data further support regular olive oil intake as part of a
healthy diet. Moreover, natural sources of tyrosol also include red and
white wines (Covas et al., 2003; Miró-Casas et al., 2001). As mentioned,
previous data indicated lower oral bioavailability of tyrosol with ad-
ministration in an aqueous solution (Tuck et al., 2001). It is thus likely
that red and white wines are less eﬀective natural sources of tyrosol
comparing to olive oils. However, red and white wines can still be good
natural sources of tyrosol, as substantial amounts of tyrosol can be
absorbed with an estimated oral bioavailability of approximate 75%
(Tuck et al., 2001). Thus, we further speculate that regular intake of
these natural sources of tyrosol may provide beneﬁcial eﬀects and mi-
tigate endotoxemia-induced organ injuries. However, additional re-
levant clinical studies are required before the beneﬁcial eﬀects of any
natural tyrosol sources can be concluded.

CD14 upregulation and subsequent inﬂammation are the crucial
mechanisms mediating the development of sepsis induced by endotoxin
(Fitzgerald et al., 2004; Jerala, 2007; Pålsson-McDermott and O'Neill,
2004; Park & Lee, 2013; Schumann, 1992). Similar to endotoxin-in-
duced sepsis, CD14 and subsequent inﬂammation has been linked to
several other diseases not mediated by the presence of endotoxin. For
instance, CD14 upregulation and inﬂammation have been noted in
cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, atherosclerosis, cor-
onary heart disease, and chronic heart failure (Anker et al., 1997;
Arroyo-Espliguero, Avanzas, Jeﬀery, & Kaski, 2004). Insulin resistance
and obesity may also be associated with CD14 upregulation and in-
ﬂammation (De Loera-Rodriguez et al., 2014; Fernández-Real et al.,
2011). The eﬀects of tyrosol on inhibiting CD14 upregulation and mi-
tigating inﬂammation were conﬁrmed in this study. Thus, tyrosol likely
has beneﬁcial eﬀects against hypertension, atherosclerosis, coronary
heart disease, chronic heart failure, insulin resistance, and obesity by
modulating CD14 upregulation and inhibiting inﬂammation. Regular
intake of natural sources of tyrosol may thus have beneﬁcial eﬀects
against these diseases. In vivo studies, employing animal models and
humans, are thus warranted.

Similar to tyrosol, hydroxytyrosol, a major polyphenol presents in
olive oil (Covas et al., 2003; Miró-Casas et al., 2001), may also possess
potent anti-inﬂammatory eﬀects (Zhang, Cao, & Zhong, 2009; Yang

et al., 2017). Hydroxytyrosol could inhibit cytokine production in LPS-
stimulated human monocytic cells (Zhang et al., 2009) and mitigate
acute lung injury in rodents with endotoxemia (Yang et al., 2017). In
addition to tyrosol and hydroxytyrosol, the anti-inﬂammatory eﬀects of
many other polyphenols have been reported. For instance, the citrus
ﬂavonoids hesperidin and hesperetin can mitigate inﬂammation
through inhibiting NF-κB, inducible nitric oxide synthase, and cy-
clooxygenase 2 (Parhiz, Roohbakhsh, Soltani, Rezaee, & Iranshahi,
2015). Common polyphenols in fruits, vegetables, and grains (e.g.,
ﬂavonol quercetin and anthocyanins) can exert substantial eﬀects on
modulating endotoxin-induced inﬂammation (Wang & Mazza, 2002). In
addition, polyphenols from cocoa and nuts can mitigate inﬂammation
and exert cardiovascular protective eﬀects (Natsume, 2018; Rusu,
Gheldiu, Mocan, Vlase, & Popa, 2018). Because polyphenols have po-
tent anti-inﬂammatory properties, regular intake of phenol-rich natural
sources (e.g., olive oil, red wine, white wine, citrus, fruits, vegetables,
grains, cocoa, nuts) may boost the endogenous anti-inﬂammatory ca-
pacity of the human body. Polyphenols thus have great therapeutic
potential. Novel therapies based on polyphenols that may provide
natural alternatives for preventing or treating inﬂammation-related
diseases should be investigated.

The above-mentioned eﬀects of tyrosol and the possible mechanisms
are summarized in Fig. 9. Nevertheless, this study has several limita-
tions. First, we used a widely used immortalised macrophage activation
model, namely the RAW264.7 cell line treated with LPS; however, the
corresponding eﬀects on primary cell cultures remain unknown.
Second, this was a cellular study; whether tyrosol can exert similar
eﬀects in intact animals remains unknown. Third, tyrosol was ad-
ministered 60 min before LPS; therefore, only the ‘pretreatment’ eﬀects
of tyrosol were observed in this study. Whether tyrosol exerts similar
eﬀects after administration at diﬀerent time points (e.g., cotreatment or
posttreatment) remains unclear. Fourth, tyrosol mitigates mCD14 ac-
tivation by enhancing protease-mediated cleavage of CD14. However,
whether tyrosol directly activates the proteases remains unclear. Fifth,
LPS exposure for 15 min could induce signiﬁcant mCD14 expression on
RAW264.7 cells; this is consistent with previous ﬁndings that LPS can
signiﬁcantly induce monocyte mCD14 upregulation (∼50% increase)
within 30 min (Marchant, Duchow, Delville, & Goldman, 1992). Be-
cause the reaction time was short, the underlying mechanisms may
involve translocation of an intracellular pool of CD14 rather than pro-
tein synthesis (Marchant et al., 1992). In addition, the current ﬁndings
indicated that tyrosol pretreatment can enhance proteolytic cleavage of
mCD14. The eﬀects of LPS on increasing mCD14 expression are likely
negated by enhanced proteolytic cleavage of mCD14 induced by tyrosol
pretreatment. Thus, our data indicate that the mCD14 expression was
signiﬁcantly lower in the LPS+T(1.2) group than that in the LPS group.
Nevertheless, this study did not investigate mCD14 source, and there-
fore, whether the increased mCD14 expression in LPS-stimulated
RAW264.7 cells is derived from translocation of an intracellular pool of
CD14 remains unclear. Sixth, the LPS-induced signalling pathway is
mediated by both the CD14-dependent and -independent pathways
(Płóciennikowska, Hromada-Judycka, Borzęcka, & Kwiatkowska,
2015). Our ﬁndings revealed that the CD14-dependent pathway is in-
volved in mediating the eﬀects of tyrosol on inhibiting the LPS-induced
signalling pathway. However, whether this eﬀect also involves the
CD14-independent pathway requires further study. Finally, although
this study indicates that tyrosol can inhibit TLR4/MD2 expression in
LPS-stimulated macrophages, direct evidence delineating the eﬀects of
tyrosol on inhibiting TLR4/MD2 dimerization and endocytosis remains
lacking.
