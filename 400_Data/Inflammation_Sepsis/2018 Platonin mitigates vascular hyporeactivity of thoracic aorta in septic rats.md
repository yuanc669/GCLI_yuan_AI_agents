Introduction

Experimental protocol

Platonin, a photosensitive dye, possesses potent anti-
oxidation and antiinﬂammation effects.1 Clinical and
experimental data have conﬁrmed the beneﬁcial effects of
platonin against conditions associated with oxidative stress
and/or inﬂammation.2-6 For instance, platonin can improve
clinical symptoms, mitigate inﬂammation severity, and
maintain the remission state in cases with juvenile
rheumatoid arthritis.2 Experimental data also conﬁrmed that
platonin can improve circulatory failure, attenuate organ
injuries, and decrease mortality in septic rats.3,4 In addition,
the injuries imposed by hemorrhage or ischemia-reperfusion
can also be attenuated by platonin.5,6

As mentioned previously, platonin improves circulatory
failure in sepsis.3 Vascular hyporeactivity contributes to
hemodynamic alterations and circulatory failure in sepsis.7
Inﬂammation and oxidative stress are two crucial mechanisms
in mediating the development of vascular hyporeactivity
induced by sepsis.8 In line with this notion, we thus speculated
that platonin may very likely mitigate vascular hyporeactivity
induced by sepsis.

To elucidate further, we thus conducted this rodent study
with the hypothesis that platonin could mitigate thoracic
aorta hyporeactivity in septic rats. This study employed a
widely used cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) polymicrobial
sepsis model9 to facilitate investigation. Vascular reactivity
was measured using an isolated thoracic aortic ring platform,
as previously reported.10 In addition, the levels of inﬂamma-
tion and oxidation of the isolated thoracic aorta were also
measured.

Methods

The animal study protocol was approved by the Animal Use
and Care Committee of Taipei Tzu Chi Hospital, Taipei, Taiwan
(105-IACUC-013). Care and handling of the animals were in
accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines. A
total of 36 adult male SpragueeDawley rats (BioLASCO, Taipei,
Taiwan; 200-250 gm) were used in this study.

performed according to previous protocols.9 In brief, all
rats were anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine mixture
(110/10 mg/kg body weight, ipf) and one 1-cm transverse
laparotomy was performed at the right lower quarter of the
abdominal wall sterilely. Then, the cecum was ligated, and
two 0.5-cm blade incisions were made followed by abdominal

another set of rats received laparotomy, cecum identiﬁcation,
and wound closure, but not CLP (i.e. sham operation). To
alleviate would pain, subcutaneous injection of 0.25%
bupivacaine (AstraZeneca, Taipei, Taiwan) was performed
before wound closure. After recovery, all rats were closely
monitored for 12 hours without restraint.

Rats were randomized to receive sham operation (Sham), Sham
plus platonin (100 mg/kg, Kankohsha Co, Osaka,
Japan;
dissolved in a mixture of 0.5 mL normal saline), CLP, or CLP plus
platonin (10, 50, or 100 mg/kg) and designated as the Sham, P,
CLP, CLP þ P(10), CLP þ P(50), and CLP þ P(100) group, respec-
tively (n ¼ 6 in each group). Platonin was injected via tail vein
immediately after CLP or at the comparable time point in the P
group. The dosages of platonin were determined according to a
previous report.3 To control for the effects of vehicle, rats of the
Sham and the CLP groups also received injection of 0.5-mL
normal saline via tail vein. At 12 hours after intervention, all
surviving rats were euthanized with decapitation.

In vitro assessment of vascular reactivity

After euthanization, thoracotomy was performed and the
descending thoracic aorta was isolated and the connective
tissues were cleaned. The distal portion of the descending
aorta was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at (cid:3)80(cid:4)C
for subsequent analysis. The proximal portion of
the
descending aorta was cut into ring-like segments (3-4 mm)
and then mounted in tissue bath chambers (Radnoti Four Unit
Tissue Organ Bath System, ADInstruments Inc. Colorado
Springs, CO) containing Krebs solution (NaCl 115.3 mM, KCl
4.9 mM, CaCl2. 2H2O 1.46 mM, MgSO4 1.2 mM, KH2PO4 1.2 mM,
NaHCO3 25 mM, d-glucose 11.1 mM; pH 7.4)11 and maintained
at 37(cid:4)C with continuously bubbled with a gas mixture of 95%
O2/5% CO2. All chemicals used for Krebs solution were
obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Reactivity of the
isolated vascular rings was measured and the tension
was recorded using isometric force transducers (MLT0420,
ADInstruments) connected to an acquisition system (Power-
Lab 16/35, ADInstruments) coupled to a recording software
(LabChart Data Analysis Software, ADInstruments).

The aortic rings were stabilized under a resting tension of
1.0 g for 60 minutes before measurement. Then the aortic rings
were exposed to one of the vasoconstrictors, phenylephrine
(1 nM to 100 mM; Sigma-Aldrich) or angiotensin II (1 nM to 100 mM;
Sigma-Aldrich), to induce contraction and the contraction
obtained was recorded. Then the vasodilator acetylcholine
(1 mM; Sigma-Aldrich) was added to induce relaxation. To
ensure the integrity of endothelium, only those aortic ring
preparations that acetylcholine could induce at least 80% relax-
ation in vasoconstrictor-induced contraction were used in this
study. Each preparation was exposed to only one vasoconstrictor
(either phenylephrine or angiotensin II). With the completion of
each cycle of contraction and relaxation, the preparations were
washed with Krebs solution and were stabilized for 30 min before
initiation of the next cycle of contraction and relaxation.
Cumulative concentration response curves to vasoconstrictors
were then constructed for vascular reactivity analysis.

Assays of nitric oxide and malondialdehyde for oxidative

Snap frozen thoracic aorta tissues were processed as above-

(reactive nitrogen species)15 and malondialdehyde (MDA) (an

thoracic aorta were measured. Commercially available color-
imetric assay kits (Nitrate/Nitrite Colorimetric Assay Kit,
Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI; Lipid Peroxidation Assay
Kit, Oxford Biomedical Research, Oxford, MI) were employed
and assays were performed according to the manufacturers’
guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Two-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnett’s multi-
ple comparison test was performed to compare the
between-group difference. All data were presented as
means (cid:5) standard deviations. The signiﬁcance level was set
as 0.05. The SPSS 16.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) was used for
statistical analyses.

Discussion

Data from the present study revealed that the maximal
contractile effects Emax and vascular reactivity of thoracic
aortic rings induced by vasoconstrictors in rats receiving CLP
were signiﬁcantly lower than those in rats receiving sham
operations. These data conﬁrmed that the CLP ploymicrobial
sepsis model employed in this study could readily induce
signiﬁcant vascular hyporeactivity in rats. Our data also
revealed that the Emax and vascular reactivity of aortic rings
induced by vasoconstrictors in rats receiving CLP plus plato-
nin (50 and 100 mg/kg) were signiﬁcantly higher than those in
rats receiving CLP. These data conﬁrmed our hypothesis and

demonstrated that platonin could attenuate the effects of
polymicrobial sepsis and mitigate vascular hyporeactivity in
rats receiving CLP. In a previous report, platonin was shown to
mitigate circulatory failure in septic rats.3 Data from this
study thus provide clear evidence to further depict the
mechanism underlying the abovementioned beneﬁcial effects
of platonin against sepsis.3 As platonin is currently used in
clinical conditions,2 impact of our data should be profound
and warrant further investigation.

Certain study limitations do exist and need to be
acknowledged. First, the effects of platonin were examined at
the early phase of sepsis (i.e. 12 hours after CLP) in this study.
The question of whether platonin can exert similar effects in
the delay phase of sepsis remains unstudied. Second, our data
revealed that platonin at the dosages of 50 and 100 mg/kg
exerted beneﬁcial effects on restoring vascular reactivity in
septic rats. However, more studies are needed before we can
conclude on the overall effects of platonin with different
dosages.

In conclusions, data from this rodent study conﬁrmed that
platonin can mitigate vascular hyporeactivity induced by
polymicrobial sepsis.
